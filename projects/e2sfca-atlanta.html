Multimodal Healthcare Accessibility (E2SFCA) — Atlanta

How many hospital beds can a household actually reach — not as the crow flies, but by however they really travel? An Enhanced Two-Step Floating Catchment Area (E2SFCA) model scoring 600 census tracts across Fulton, DeKalb, and Clayton counties by drive, transit, and walk access to hospital beds.

Type
Master’s thesis research (TFM)
Year
2026
Role
Sole researcher
Study area
Fulton, DeKalb & Clayton counties, metro Atlanta — 600 census tracts
Tools
ArcGIS Pro, Network Analyst, Python
Data
US Census ACS, CMS hospital provider data (bed counts), GTFS transit schedules, street network

Objective

Most healthcare-accessibility maps assume everyone drives. That systematically overstates access for car-free households and hides where transit and walking are actually doing the work — or failing to. This project built a multimodal accessibility surface that scores each tract by the mode(s) its residents actually use, to surface where hospital-bed access is genuinely thin versus merely far from a highway.

Method

The model follows the multimodal Enhanced Two-Step Floating Catchment Area method (E2SFCA) described by Mao & Nekorchuk (2013), extending the original 2SFCA with distance decay and multiple travel modes instead of a single catchment radius.

Step 1 — supply-to-demand ratio

For every hospital, a catchment is drawn per mode (drive, transit, walk) using the travel times below. Within each catchment, its bed supply is divided by the population reachable within that same catchment (also weighted by distance decay), giving a supply-to-demand ratio for each mode.

Catchments & distance decay

Rather than a hard cutoff, each mode uses a tiered weighting scheme approximating a Gaussian decay — closer is weighted higher, and the weight steps down in stages out to the catchment’s edge:

ModeTime tiers (weight)
Drive≤10 min (1.0) · ≤20 min (0.6) · ≤30 min (0.2) · >30 min (0)
Transit≤15 min (1.0) · ≤30 min (0.7) · ≤45 min (0.4) · ≤60 min (0.1) · >60 min (0)
Walk≤10 min (1.0) · ≤20 min (0.6) · ≤30 min (0.2) · >30 min (0)

Step 2 — accessibility at each tract

Each tract sums the (decay-weighted) supply-to-demand ratios of every hospital within reach, separately per mode. The three mode-specific scores are then combined into a single multimodal index, weighted by that tract’s own local mode share — so a tract where a third of residents commute by transit has its transit-mode score properly represented in its final score, rather than averaged away against two modes fewer of its residents use.

The three mode-specific scores are also available on their own (use the toggle above the map). Each represents the competitive bed accessibility available to a tract by that mode alone — still accounting for cross-modal competition for the same beds — rather than what a resident would see if the whole population switched to it.

Results

The final index ranges from 0 to 5.3 beds per 1,000 residents across the three counties (mean 2.67). Clayton County comes out clearly underserved — a mean of 1.37, versus 2.90 in DeKalb and 2.79 in Fulton. Access also rises with income: the highest-income tercile of tracts averages 2.98 versus 2.35 in the lowest. But the multimodal design surfaces a less obvious pattern too: tracts with little or no vehicle access do not score worse on average (2.86 vs. 2.39 for the lowest car-free tercile) — because those tracts concentrate in the urban core, where transit and shorter walk distances substantially offset the lack of a car. A drive-only model would have missed that entirely.

Recommendations

Target any near-term intervention — new transit routes, mobile clinics — at Clayton County first, given its clearly lower mean score; and, because car-free tracts are not uniformly underserved, prioritize by tract-level accessibility score rather than by vehicle-ownership rate alone.

Possible extensions

  • Extend the study area to the full 13-county Atlanta Regional Commission boundary.
  • Weight hospitals by service type (e.g. trauma level, maternity) rather than total bed count alone.
  • Validate the modeled catchments against real patient-origin data where available.
  • Add a bike-mode catchment alongside drive/transit/walk.

Seven tracts (mostly zero-population airport/industrial tracts) have no computable score and are shown in gray. Citation: Mao, L., & Nekorchuk, D. (2013). Measuring spatial accessibility to healthcare for populations with multiple transportation modes. Health & Place, 24, 115–122.
