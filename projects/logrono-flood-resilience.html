Fluvial Flood Risk & Resilience — Logroño

A hazard-to-risk pipeline for a 500-year fluvial flood scenario (T500) in Logroño, Spain: flood severity, land-cover and road exposure, a national urban vulnerability index, and a final composite risk map — with a concrete drainage recommendation for the most exposed neighborhood.

Type
Master’s coursework deliverable
Year
2025 – 2026
Role
Sole author
Study area
Municipio de Logroño, La Rioja — 79 km², ~152,000 inhabitants
Tools
ArcGIS Pro, Spatial Analyst
Data
T500 flood-hazard model, Urban Atlas land cover, INE census sections, national ISVUN-G vulnerability index

Objective

A flood-hazard map shows where the water goes; it doesn’t show who is most exposed to the consequences. This project builds the full pipeline from a T500 (500-year return period) flood-severity model through land-cover and road-network exposure to a national urban-vulnerability index, combining hazard and vulnerability into a single composite risk map that can actually target a resilience intervention.

Method

The T500 flood-severity model (five depth-based classes, from a hydrological simulation of the Ebro and Iregua rivers) was intersected with Urban Atlas land cover and the municipal road network to quantify physical exposure. Separately, the national ISVUN-G (Índice Sintético de Vulnerabilidad Urbana Nacional — Global) index — a pre-existing composite of social, economic, and environmental indicators published at census-section level — was mapped across the municipality. The final risk layer combines the two: each zone’s risk class reflects both how severe the flood hazard is and how vulnerable the population living there already is, rather than hazard alone.

Results

Severity follows a continuous corridor along the riverbed and its immediate floodplain, with the most affected areas north of the Ebro — the historic core and industrial zones to the east — and a second, less extreme zone around the Ebro–Iregua confluence. Most of the exposed land cover is arable land (46%) and pasture/permanent crops (a further 24%), which limits the population directly affected, but 88 ha of road network and roughly 4.0 km² of combined urban/infrastructural land is also exposed.

Barrio San Antonio — north of the Ebro, facing the historic core — carries the most exposed road network of any neighborhood, consistent with its denser, more traditional urban fabric. It also scores very high on the ISVUN-G vulnerability index, compounding its exposure. At the municipal scale, the final risk map concentrates around three distinct zones along the river corridor: the San Antonio residential area, the largely agricultural zone around Pasada Canicalejo to the southeast, and the Polígono Industrial Cantabria to the northeast — each implying a different kind of resilience response given how differently that land is used.

Recommendations

Given San Antonio’s combination of high road exposure, high vulnerability, and proximity to the historic core, prioritize it for a concrete intervention: sustainable urban drainage systems (SUDS) integrated into buildings and streets to manage runoff effectively. The neighborhood’s susceptibility to standing water is compounded by its scarcity of permeable, unbuilt land nearby that could otherwise absorb it.

Possible extensions

  • Repeat the analysis for less extreme but more frequent return periods (T100, T10) to see how the risk footprint changes.
  • Model the risk-reduction effect of the recommended SUDS intervention directly, rather than proposing it only qualitatively.
  • Incorporate real-time river-gauge data to move from a static return-period model toward an early-warning application.

Severity and risk are shown as a 50 m majority-class grid, aggregated from a much finer raw hazard model so the full municipal extent loads quickly in a browser; vulnerability is shown at its native census-section resolution.
